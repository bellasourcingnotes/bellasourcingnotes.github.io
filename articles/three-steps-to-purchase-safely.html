Buying Process

Three Steps to Purchase Safely From China

By Bella Cheng·6 min read·Shenzhen, China

Most sourcing problems do not begin on the shipment date. They begin earlier, when requirements are vague, verification is treated as a formality or payment moves faster than the available evidence.

A safe process does not eliminate every risk. It creates better decision points before the cost of changing direction becomes high.

1. Define what “acceptable” means

A product name is not a complete requirement. Before asking suppliers for quotations, write down the details that will later determine whether the order is usable.

  • Application and operating environment
  • Material, dimensions, tolerance and finish
  • Quantity for samples, pilot order and expected repeat orders
  • Required documents, labels, packaging and test evidence
  • Target timing and the consequences of delay
  • Which details are fixed and which may be adjusted

When these points remain unclear, suppliers make assumptions. Different assumptions create quotations that look comparable but are not.

2. Verify the supplier against the requirement

Business registration is useful, but it is only one layer. A legitimate company may still be the wrong supplier for your product. Verification should focus on the capability needed for this specific order.

  • Does the supplier understand the requirement without repeatedly avoiding technical questions?
  • Can it show relevant equipment, process steps, samples or test records?
  • Is the quoted lead time consistent with the actual process?
  • Who will be responsible after payment?
  • Are company name, bank details, quotation and invoice consistent?
Field note

A polished website proves that a supplier has a polished website. It does not prove that the factory can control your critical requirement.

3. Check before the balance and shipment

The last useful moment to find a problem is before the goods leave. The check should be based on the original requirement, not on a generic collection of photographs.

Depending on the order, useful evidence may include quantity, appearance, dimensions, function, labels, packaging, serial numbers, nameplates, documents and a short operation or test video. Any issue should be recorded clearly, with responsibility and the next action agreed before shipment.

The real purpose of the three steps

Definition, verification and pre-shipment checking form a connected process. Each stage improves the next one. The goal is not paperwork for its own sake; it is to replace avoidable assumptions with evidence while there is still time to act.

This article provides general sourcing information, not legal, regulatory or product-specific compliance advice. The right checks depend on the product, market and transaction.
